\section{Summary of Contributions and Technical Milestones}
Managing Type 2 Diabetes is challenging because two people eating the exact same meal can experience completely different blood glucose spikes. General dietary guidelines cannot account for these personal differences. In this thesis, we built \textbf{GlycoTwin}, a personalized multi-modal digital twin framework that predicts continuous 2-hour blood glucose trajectories after meals and lets users test what-if scenarios before eating.

The key milestones and contributions of our work are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Synchronized Multi-Modal Dataset:} We aligned several data sources from 45 participants in the CGMacros study (covering 1,633 meal events). This included continuous glucose monitoring data (from Abbott FreeStyle Libre and Dexcom G6 sensors), wearable activity metrics from Fitbit (heart rate and METs), logged meal macronutrients, laboratory blood tests, and gut microbiome profiling.
    \item \textbf{Microbiome Dimensionality Reduction with $\beta$-VAE:} We trained an unsupervised $\beta$-Variational Autoencoder to compress 1,979 sparse bacterial taxa into an 8-dimensional continuous latent space ($\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{8}$). By applying input masking noise and $\beta$-annealing, the autoencoder learned meaningful microbial representations without overfitting, achieving an empirical reconstruction ROC-AUC and PR-AUC of 1.0000.
    \item \textbf{TransformerForecaster Model Architecture:} We developed a neural network architecture designed specifically for this multi-modal task. It uses multi-head self-attention to process pre-meal dynamic sequences, a Gated Residual Network (GRN) to filter static patient and meal features, a residual delta formulation ($\Delta G_t = G_t - G_0$) to remove baseline offset bias, and a composite loss function to penalize peak height and rate-of-change errors.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation on Unseen Participants:} We tested our models using 5-fold cross-validation where no participant was shared between training and test sets. On unseen individuals, TransformerForecaster achieved an MAE of \textbf{19.37 mg/dL}, an RMSE of \textbf{28.14 mg/dL}, a Peak MAE of \textbf{24.73 mg/dL}, and an $R^2$ of \textbf{0.688}. This improved over recurrent baselines (LSTM and GRU) by 30.4\% and outperformed the published CGMacros gradient boosting baseline by 54.7\%.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Safety Verification:} On the Clarke Error Grid, \textbf{98.47\%} of all predictions fell within clinically safe Zones A and B, with zero points in dangerous error Zones D or E, meeting clinical safety criteria (ISO 15197 / IEC).
    \item \textbf{Causal Simulation Engine and Mobile App:} We built an interactive what-if simulation tool that evaluates four behavioral options (such as cutting carbs or taking a 30-minute walk) and ranks them using a Clinical Utility Score ($U \in [0, 1]$). We also developed a lightweight mobile web app so users can run these simulations on their phones before meals.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Clinical and Practical Implications}
Moving from standard reactive glucose tracking to a predictive digital twin offers several practical advantages for diabetes care:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Proactive Decision-Making Before Meals:} Traditional CGMs only alert patients after glucose has already spiked. With GlycoTwin, patients can see the predicted outcome before they start eating. Showing that a short 30-minute walk can lower the expected rise from 48.2 mg/dL to 6.3 mg/dL gives users practical motivation to build healthy habits.
    \item \textbf{Going Beyond Simple Carb Counting:} By factoring in insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and gut microbiome traits alongside meal nutrients, the model explains why different individuals react differently to the same food, offering truly personalized guidance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations of the Current Study}
Although the results are promising, our study has a few limitations that should be noted:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Self-Reported Meal Logging:} While meal logs recorded nutrient weights, participants logged their meals in free-living conditions, which can introduce small estimation errors in portion sizes.
    \item \textbf{Sensor Time Delay:} Continuous glucose monitors measure glucose in interstitial fluid instead of venous blood. This causes an unavoidable physiological delay of about 5 to 10 minutes when glucose is rising quickly.
    \item \textbf{Cohort Size and Demographics:} The CGMacros dataset contains 45 participants from Portugal. While 1,633 meal events were sufficient to train and evaluate our models, testing on larger and more diverse populations with varied diets and genetics will be helpful in future work.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope for Further Research}
There are several directions where this work can be extended:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty Estimation with Conformal Prediction:} Future work can use conformal prediction to provide prediction intervals (such as a 95\% confidence band) around each forecasted curve. This would show users when the model is less confident about an unfamiliar meal.
    \item \textbf{Connecting with Smart Insulin Pens:} Combining GlycoTwin's trajectory forecasts with connected smart insulin pens could assist insulin-dependent patients in choosing appropriate mealtime bolus doses.
    \item \textbf{Tracking Microbiome Changes Over Time:} Monitoring how a patient's gut microbiome changes across months of dietary changes could allow the digital twin to update its internal parameters as the gut flora evolves.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Trials in Real-World Settings:} Conducting prospective clinical trials comparing patients using GlycoTwin with patients using standard CGM alarms could measure the real-world impact on Time-in-Range and long-term HbA1c levels.
\end{enumerate}
